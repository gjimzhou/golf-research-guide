# Wind, weather, and course-condition planning

Destination golf is highly sensitive to conditions. A famous course on the wrong day can be worse value than a less celebrated course played in good weather and normal maintenance condition.

## 1. Separate weather from course condition

These are related but different.

### Weather
- wind speed and gusts;
- rain probability and intensity;
- temperature;
- humidity;
- lightning risk;
- sunrise and sunset.

### Course condition
- recent rainfall;
- drainage;
- firmness;
- aeration;
- topdressing;
- temporary greens;
- cart-path-only restrictions;
- storm damage;
- bunker condition.

A dry forecast does not imply a dry course.

## 2. Wind matters more than the headline temperature

For exposed coastal and island courses, wind can dominate difficulty.

High wind changes:
- carry distance;
- dispersion;
- club selection;
- trajectory;
- pace of play;
- whether scenic holes remain enjoyable.

Use **sustained wind and gusts**, not only a generic “windy” label.

## 3. Direction matters

The same wind speed can produce very different rounds depending on routing.

If the course has exposed ocean holes, record:
- prevailing wind direction;
- orientation of signature holes;
- whether the hardest holes play into the wind;
- whether afternoon trade winds are common.

A course may be strategically designed around a prevailing wind, but unusual direction can make it play very differently.

## 4. Rainfall has lagged effects

Recent rain can matter after the storm has passed.

Potential effects:
- little or no rollout;
- plugged balls;
- slower greens;
- cart restrictions;
- muddy rough;
- longer effective yardage;
- slower pace.

For a high-value tee time, check both forecast and prior 24–72 hour rainfall where practical.

## 5. Maintenance risk is first-class

Aeration and topdressing can materially alter putting quality even when the course remains open.

Before booking or before a cancellation deadline, verify:
- aeration dates;
- recovery window;
- temporary greens;
- overseeding / grow-in periods where applicable;
- major construction;
- storm-related closures.

The relevant question is not merely “is the course open?” but “is the experience normal enough to justify the price?”

## 6. Cart restrictions alter the itinerary

Cart-path-only can add significant walking and time.

Consequences may include:
- slower rounds;
- higher fatigue;
- less attractive back-to-back activities;
- greater hydration needs;
- more difficulty for players with mobility limitations.

On resort trips, this can affect dinner reservations, spa bookings, flights, and hotel transfers.

## 7. Build a weather threshold before the trip

Avoid making the decision emotionally on the morning of play.

Example framework:

### Green
- normal maintenance;
- manageable wind;
- low lightning risk;
- no major standing-water issue.

### Yellow
- moderate rain risk;
- strong but playable wind;
- recent aeration with substantial recovery;
- cart-path-only.

### Red
- lightning;
- severe wind;
- active heavy rain;
- major temporary-green use;
- known closure or unsafe conditions.

Thresholds should reflect the course price, cancellation policy, and how replaceable the round is.

## 8. Morning vs. afternoon

Morning often offers:
- calmer wind;
- firmer schedule certainty;
- more daylight buffer.

Afternoon may offer:
- lower rates;
- better light;
- attractive sunset finishing holes.

The correct choice depends on local wind cycle, sunset, pace, and cancellation economics.

## 9. Forecast uncertainty

A forecast several days out should be treated as a distribution, not a fact.

As the tee time approaches:
- update wind and precipitation;
- check course notices;
- review local radar / short-term forecast;
- confirm maintenance and cart status.

Do not overreact to a single long-range icon.

## 10. Record operational facts with dates

Course-condition notes age quickly. A useful research note should include:

- source;
- date checked;
- condition observed or announced;
- whether it is temporary;
- when re-verification is needed.

## Decision rule

For destination golf, maximize **expected round quality**, not prestige alone:

```
expected round quality =
course quality
× probability of normal conditions
- logistics / cancellation friction
```

A lower-ranked course in excellent condition can be the better decision than a marquee course during aeration, storm recovery, or extreme wind.
